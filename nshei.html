Dvar Torah · Parshas Eikev

Why Hashem Commands Us to Love the Stranger

Tucked into Parshas Eikev is one of the most striking mitzvos in the entire Torah: love the ger, because you yourselves were strangers in Mitzrayim. What does it actually ask of us?

Why Hashem Commands Us to Love the Stranger

"And you shall love the ger, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt." Of all the reasons the Torah could give for a mitzvah, this one is deeply personal — remember what it felt like to not belong.

In the middle of Parshas Eikev, right after describing Hashem's own love — "He loves the ger, giving him bread and clothing" — the Torah turns to us: "V'ahavtem es hager, ki gerim heyisem b'eretz Mitzrayim." Love the stranger, because you were strangers in the land of Egypt.

Most mitzvos are given simply as Divine command. This one comes with a memory attached. Hashem isn't only telling us what to do; He's reminding us how it felt to be on the outside — friendless, foreign, without status or a place at anyone's table.

🚪 A Mitzvah Built on Memory

Our sages count this command, in its various forms, among the most repeated in the entire Torah — a sign of how easily it's forgotten once we're no longer the ones on the outside. Comfort has a way of erasing memory. Once we have a home, a community, a table of our own, it's tempting to lose touch with what it felt like not to.

That's precisely why the Torah insists on it here, in Eikev, a parsha all about not forgetting — not forgetting the desert, not forgetting Who gave us our blessings, and, it turns out, not forgetting where we came from.

🍽️ What It Looks Like at Home

Practically, this mitzvah doesn't require a stranger at the border. It shows up much closer to home: the new family in shul who doesn't know anyone yet. The classmate who eats lunch alone. The neighbor who just moved from out of town and hasn't been invited anywhere. Loving the ger, in its quietest form, is simply refusing to let anyone feel like an outsider at your table.

Every woman who's ever set an extra place, walked over to introduce herself first, or made sure the new girl had a seat, has fulfilled this mitzvah in its most literal sense — using her own memory of not belonging to make sure someone else never has to feel it.

May we always remember what it felt like to be the stranger — and let that memory turn into someone else's welcome. 🏡

Small tricks, big difference

Kitchen & Home Tips

Little hacks passed down from Bubbies, neighbors, and years of trial & error. Send us yours — we'll add them to the collection.

🥑
Produce hack

Keep cut avocado from browning

Squeeze fresh lemon juice over the cut surface, then press plastic wrap directly onto the flesh — no air, no brown. Stays green for a full day.

🧁
Make-ahead

Freeze muffin batter, bake fresh

Portion unbaked muffin or coffee cake batter into a lined tin and freeze. Bake straight from frozen (add 5-8 extra minutes) for oven-fresh Shabbos morning treats.

🍯
Pantry fix

Soften hardened brown sugar

Tuck a slice of plain bread into the bag or container and seal overnight. The sugar pulls moisture from the bread and softens right up.

🥚
Kitchen trick

Peel hard-boiled eggs in seconds

Shock eggs in an ice bath right after boiling, then gently roll each one on the counter to crackle the shell all over. It slips off in one or two pieces.

🥗
Make-ahead

Build a Shabbos salad in a jar

Layer dressing on the bottom, sturdy vegetables next, and greens on top in a mason jar. Keeps crisp in the fridge for days — just shake and pour before serving.

🔪
Safety tip

Stop a cutting board from sliding

Place a damp paper towel underneath the board before you start chopping. It grips the counter and keeps everything steady.

🧅
No more tears

Chill onions before chopping

Pop onions in the freezer for 15 minutes before cutting. The cold slows the release of the compound that makes your eyes water.

🗄️
Erev Shabbos

Lazy susans for the fridge door

Group condiments and small jars on a lazy susan on your fridge shelf. One spin finds anything — nothing gets lost or forgotten before candle lighting.

For the little ones

Kids' Corner — No Electronics Needed

Ideas to save your sanity on long summer afternoons and hot Shabbos weekends. Grouped by situation — pick a tab, pick an idea, and go.

All ages

Memory tray challenge

Arrange 12 household items on a tray. Let everyone study it for one minute, then cover it up. Go around calling out items from memory — last one to forget wins.

Ages 5+

Bracha bee

Call out a food, kids race to say the correct bracha out loud. Get it right, you stay in; miss one, you sit out a round. Last one standing wins.

Ages 4+

Human knot

Everyone stands in a circle and grabs two different hands across the group. Working together (talking only, no letting go!) untangle back into one big circle.

Ages 3+

Balloon keep-it-up

Blow up a balloon before Shabbos starts. See how long the whole family can keep it from touching the floor — no hands allowed for the littlest ones is extra fun.

All ages

Backyard ice hunt

Freeze small toys in ice cube trays. Kids "excavate" them with spoons and warm water. Cools them down and buys you 45 minutes.

Ages 3+

Sidewalk chalk mural

Give each kid a section of the sidewalk. When done, walk the whole family through the "art gallery" and give awards.

Ages 5+

Lemonade stand

Set up out front. Real money, real customers. Kids learn making change, customer service, and marketing. Great mitzvah opportunity: donate to tzedakah.

All ages

The nature bingo card

Make a bingo card of things to find outside: red leaf, feather, three types of rocks, a bug, a puddle. Walk the block. First to bingo wins.

Ages 4+

Backyard obstacle course

Chairs, cushions, hula hoops, a broomstick. Have older kids design it. Time each other. Bonus: video the runs.

All ages

Sprinkler paintbrush

Give kids paintbrushes and buckets of water. Let them "paint" the fence, the driveway, the siding. Water evaporates — endless canvas.

Ages 4+

Sock puppet theater

Old socks + markers + buttons + yarn. Kids make puppets, then perform a show for the family after supper. Curtains: a blanket over two chairs.

Ages 3+

Restaurant at home

Kids write "menus," set the table, take orders. Serve real snacks. Amazing pretend-play. Great vocabulary building.

All ages

Fort under the dining table

Sheets, chairs, cushions, flashlights. Read books inside. Serve lunch in the fort as a special treat. Instant magic.

Ages 5+

Cardboard box marble maze

Cut open a cereal box or shoe box. Kids glue in cardboard pieces and straws to make a marble maze. Design + engineering + hours of play.

Ages 3+

Sensory bin

Big plastic tub + rice or dry beans + small toys hidden inside + scoops and cups. Kids can play for an hour. Contain to one room!

Ages 6+

Kid Iron Chef

Give kids 3 safe ingredients from the pantry. They "compete" to make a snack. You judge. Winner gets to pick supper for that night.

Ages 1-3

Muffin tin sort

Give a muffin tin and a bowl of pom-poms, cheerios, or small blocks. Toddler "sorts" by color into the tin. 30 quiet minutes.

Ages 2-4

Water pouring station

Two cups, one bowl of water, tray underneath. Toddler pours water back and forth. Improves fine motor. Bring towels.

Ages 1-3

Sticker page

Sheet of stickers + blank paper. Toddlers can peel and stick for a shockingly long time. Buy stickers in bulk on Amazon.

Ages 2+

Play-dough tray

Homemade play-dough (flour, salt, water, food color) + cookie cutters + a rolling pin. Set them up at the table. Save the tray for next time.

Ages 1-2

Basket of "safe" kitchen tools

Wooden spoons, plastic measuring cups, silicone spatulas, small pot. Give them their own drawer in the kitchen. Endless fascination.

Ages 2-4

Painter's tape road map

Painter's tape on the floor becomes roads. Toy cars zoom around. Easy to peel up after. Endless configurations.

This week's portion, in brief

Parsha Short — Eikev

Devarim 7:12 – 11:25

Small Steps, Big Reward

Moshe continues his closing address, promising Bnei Yisrael that if they carefully keep even the mitzvos people are tempted to "trample with their heel" — the small, unglamorous ones nobody's watching for — Hashem will bless them abundantly in the Land. That word, eikev ("heel" or "because"), gives the parsha its name and its theme: consistency in the little things matters as much as the big, dramatic ones.

Moshe recalls the forty years in the desert, and the test of the manna — teaching that "man does not live on bread alone, but on every utterance from the mouth of Hashem." He also warns of the opposite danger: prosperity. When the land is good and the home is full, it's easy to let your heart grow proud and forget Who provided it all.

Eikev is also the source of the second paragraph of Shema, and of the mitzvah of Birkas Hamazon — because after we eat and are satisfied, we're commanded to notice, and to say thank you.

“And you shall eat, and be satisfied, and bless Hashem your G-d.”Devarim 8:10

Halachos of Shabbos

39 Melachos Follow-Up

Borer & Your Morning Coffee

This week's 39 Melachos installment covers Borer — but one of the most common places it comes up is the coffee pot. A drip coffee filter is generally permitted on Shabbos: you place the grounds in the filter and pour hot water through, since the water passing through is what does the separating, not an active borer motion on your part.

A French press works differently. Pressing the plunger down is your own action separating the grounds from the liquid using a tool designed for that purpose — which is exactly the problem of Borer using an instrument, and is not permitted on Shabbos.

Either way, the water itself needs to come from a permitted source (such as a kli shlishi, or an urn designed for Shabbos use), since making coffee can also raise questions of bishul (cooking) in addition to Borer. Ask your Rav about your specific hot water setup.

39 Melachos — Week 2: Borer

The Melacha of Selecting

Borer: Separating Good from Bad

Borer means taking something out of a mixture and setting it aside — the classic example is separating unwanted stones or chaff from grain. In the Mishkan, this was done while preparing dyes: after threshing, any remaining pebbles or debris had to be picked out by hand. That simple act of sorting is why Borer appears on the list of 39 melachos, and why so many everyday kitchen moments touch on it.

To separate something on Shabbos without violating Borer, three conditions must all be met: you're taking the food you want away from what you don't want (not the reverse), you're doing it by hand (not with a strainer, sieve, or other tool made for separating), and it's for immediate use — right before eating, not to set aside for later.

A few familiar examples: when eating fish, it's best to remove the bones from your mouth as you go, or eat around them, rather than picking bones out of the fish first. Watermelon seeds should be spit out while eating, or shaken/picked out right before a bite, not sorted out in advance. A teabag should be lifted out of a cup with a spoon, not squeezed and set aside. In each case, the same three conditions decide whether it's permitted.

Sourced from the Mishna Berurah, Shemiras Shabbos Kehilchasa, and contemporary poskim. This is a general overview for learning purposes — for any specific situation, please ask your own Rav.

Orit Lender — 25 Years Building Staten Island's Jewish Community

Orit Lender was born to Israeli parents and lived on Kibbutz Urim before moving to Staten Island as a child. Twenty-five years ago she started at the Joan & Alan Bernikow JCC of Staten Island as Youth Empowerment Director; she went on to build the JCC's senior services, and in 2020 — in the middle of the pandemic — she became the JCC's first-ever female CEO.

Under her leadership, the JCC partnered with UJA-Federation to launch Hillel of Staten Island for students at Wagner College and the College of Staten Island, expanded programs for people with disabilities and older adults, and led the community's food and vaccination response through COVID. In 2024, she received both the Staten Island Chamber of Commerce's Louis Miller Award and the Staten Island Economic Development Corporation's Executive Woman of the Year Award. She and her husband Ed, married 24 years, are raising two sons on Staten Island — and Orit says the whole of her work is driven by the Jewish value of tikkun olam, repairing the world one relationship at a time.

See the Choice, Choose Today

Parshas Re'eh opens with Moshe telling Klal Yisrael, "Re'eh Anochi nosein lifneichem hayom bracha uklalah" — See, I am placing before you today a blessing and a curse. The key word is "today": this wasn't a one-time decision made back at Har Sinai, it's a fresh choice offered every single day. Kids don't need to feel stuck being defined by yesterday's mistake or yesterday's bad mood. This week, try asking at bedtime: "What's one bracha you chose today?" — it reframes the whole day around the good choices, not just what went wrong.

Gulyás

Hungarian Goulash · Meat

The original one-pot Hungarian classic: tender cubes of beef slow-simmered with onions, potatoes, carrots, and plenty of sweet paprika into a rich, hearty stew. Fully meat, with no dairy anywhere near the pot.

Type Meat
Prep 20 mins
Cook 1 hour 45 mins
Yields 6 Servings
Ingredients
  • 2½ lbs Beef chuck or stew meat, cut into 1-inch cubes
  • 3 tbsp Neutral oil
  • 2 large Onions, chopped
  • 3 tbsp Sweet Hungarian paprika
  • 2 cloves Garlic, minced
  • 2 Bell peppers, chopped
  • 2 medium Tomatoes, diced (or 2 tbsp tomato paste)
  • 6 cups Beef or vegetable broth
  • 4 medium Potatoes, peeled and cubed
  • 2 medium Carrots, sliced
  • 2 tsp Salt, plus more to taste
  • ¼ tsp Black pepper
Instructions
  1. Brown the Beef. Heat the oil in a large Dutch oven or heavy pot over medium-high heat. Working in batches, sear the beef cubes on all sides until browned. Remove and set aside.
  2. Build the Base. In the same pot, add the onions and cook 6-8 minutes until soft and golden. Stir in the garlic and cook 1 minute more.
  3. Bloom the Paprika. Remove the pot from the heat briefly, stir in the paprika, then return to low heat and stir for 30 seconds until fragrant — be careful not to let it scorch.
  4. Simmer. Return the beef to the pot along with the bell peppers, tomatoes, broth, salt, and pepper. Bring to a boil, then reduce heat to low, cover, and simmer for 1 hour, stirring occasionally.
  5. Add the Vegetables. Stir in the potatoes and carrots. Cover and continue simmering for 30-40 minutes, until the meat and vegetables are completely tender. Adjust salt to taste and serve hot in deep bowls.

Csirke Paprikás

Chicken Paprikash · Meat

Hungary's most famous chicken dish, kept simple and fully meat — no dairy substitutes needed. Chicken thighs braise low and slow in a rich, paprika-red onion sauce until completely tender. Serve over egg noodles or rice.

Type Meat
Prep 15 mins
Cook 45 mins
Yields 4-6 Servings
Ingredients
  • 3 lbs Bone-in chicken thighs, skin removed
  • 2 medium Yellow onions, finely chopped
  • 3 tbsp Neutral oil
  • 3 tbsp Sweet Hungarian paprika
  • 1 medium Tomato, diced (or 1 tbsp tomato paste)
  • 1 medium Green bell pepper, sliced
  • 2 cups Chicken broth
  • 1 tbsp Flour (to thicken)
  • 1½ tsp Salt, plus more to taste
  • ¼ tsp Black pepper
Instructions
  1. Build the Base. Heat the oil in a large Dutch oven or heavy pot over medium heat. Add the onions and cook for 6-8 minutes until soft and golden. Add the diced tomato and bell pepper, and cook for another 3 minutes.
  2. Bloom the Paprika. Remove the pot from the heat for a moment (this prevents the paprika from scorching), stir in the sweet paprika, then return to low heat and stir for 30 seconds until fragrant.
  3. Braise the Chicken. Add the chicken thighs, chicken broth, salt, and pepper. Bring to a gentle simmer, cover, and cook on low heat for 30-35 minutes, until the chicken is fork-tender.
  4. Thicken the Sauce. Remove the chicken to a plate. Whisk the flour with a few tablespoons of the hot sauce in a small bowl until smooth, then whisk this back into the pot. Simmer 2-3 minutes until slightly thickened.
  5. Finish. Return the chicken to the pot and spoon the sauce over the top. Simmer 5 more minutes, then serve hot over egg noodles or rice.

Töltött Paprika

Stuffed Peppers · Meat

Sweet bell peppers stuffed with seasoned ground beef and rice, simmered low and slow in a light paprika-tomato sauce. A meat classic with no dairy needed — and it freezes beautifully for busy weeknights.

Type Meat
Prep 25 mins
Cook 1 hour
Yields 6 Servings
Ingredients
  • 6 medium Bell peppers (any color), tops cut off and seeded
  • 1½ lbs Ground beef
  • ½ cup Uncooked white rice
  • 1 medium Onion, finely chopped (divided)
  • 1 large Egg
  • 1 tsp Sweet Hungarian paprika (for filling)
  • 2 tsp Salt (divided)
  • ¼ tsp Black pepper
  • 1 can (28 oz) Crushed tomatoes
  • 2 cups Water or vegetable broth
  • 1 tbsp Sweet Hungarian paprika (for sauce)
  • 1 tbsp Sugar
  • 2 tbsp Neutral oil
Instructions
  1. Mix the Filling. In a large bowl, combine the ground beef, uncooked rice, half the chopped onion, the egg, 1 teaspoon paprika, 1 teaspoon salt, and black pepper. Mix gently just until combined.
  2. Stuff the Peppers. Fill each hollowed pepper about ¾ full with the meat mixture (the rice will expand as it cooks). Set aside.
  3. Build the Sauce. Heat the oil in a large pot over medium heat. Add the remaining chopped onion and cook 5 minutes until soft. Stir in the crushed tomatoes, water or broth, remaining tablespoon of paprika, sugar, and remaining teaspoon of salt.
  4. Simmer. Bring the sauce to a gentle boil, then carefully nestle the stuffed peppers upright into the pot. Spoon some sauce over the tops. Cover and simmer on low heat for 50-60 minutes, until the peppers are tender and the rice inside is fully cooked.
  5. Serve. Ladle peppers into bowls with plenty of the tomato sauce spooned over top. Leftovers freeze well for up to 3 months.

Lecsó

Pepper, Tomato & Onion Stew · Parve

Hungary's answer to ratatouille: sweet bell peppers, ripe tomatoes, and onion, slow-cooked together with paprika until silky. Naturally parve, it's a light, vegetable-forward side that pairs beautifully with either a meat or dairy meal — and it's a wonderful way to use up peak-summer produce.

Type Parve
Prep 15 mins
Cook 30 mins
Yields 4-6 Servings
Ingredients
  • 3 tbsp Neutral oil
  • 2 medium Onions, sliced
  • 6 medium Bell or Hungarian wax peppers, sliced into strips
  • 4 medium Ripe tomatoes, diced (or 1 can diced tomatoes)
  • 1 tbsp Sweet Hungarian paprika
  • 1 tsp Salt
  • ¼ tsp Black pepper
  • 1 tsp Sugar (to balance acidity)
Instructions
  1. Sauté the Onions. Heat the oil in a large skillet or pot over medium heat. Add the sliced onions and cook for 6-8 minutes until soft and translucent.
  2. Add the Peppers. Stir in the sliced peppers and cook for 8-10 minutes, until they begin to soften and release their juices.
  3. Bloom the Paprika. Remove the pot from the heat briefly, stir in the paprika, then return to low heat and stir for 30 seconds until fragrant — take care not to let it burn.
  4. Simmer. Add the diced tomatoes, salt, black pepper, and sugar. Stir well, cover, and simmer on low heat for 15-20 minutes, until the vegetables are soft and the mixture is thick and stew-like.
  5. Serve. Enjoy warm as a side dish, spooned over rice, or alongside eggs for a light supper. Keeps well in the fridge for up to 4 days.

Gesztenye

Hungarian Chestnut Dessert · Parve, with Dairy Option

A beloved Hungarian classic: sweetened chestnut purée pressed through a ricer into delicate strands, piled over a cloud of whipped topping. As written it's parve, so it works after any meal — and for a dairy Shabbos afternoon spread, simply swap in real whipped cream for an extra-special treat.

Type Parve (Dairy option below)
Prep 25 mins
Chill 1 hour
Yields 6 Servings
Ingredients
  • 1 lb Cooked, peeled chestnuts (vacuum-packed or jarred)
  • ½ cup Sugar, plus more to taste
  • ¾ cup Water
  • 1 tsp Vanilla extract
  • 1 tbsp Dark rum (optional)
  • 1½ cups Non-dairy whipped topping, prepared (or 1½ cups heavy cream, whipped with 2 tbsp sugar, for a dairy version)
  • For garnish: Shaved chocolate or a light dusting of cocoa
Instructions
  1. Simmer the Chestnuts. Combine the chestnuts, sugar, and water in a saucepan. Simmer over medium-low heat for 15-20 minutes, until the chestnuts are very soft and most of the liquid has absorbed.
  2. Purée. Transfer the chestnuts to a food processor with the vanilla and rum (if using). Blend until completely smooth, adding a splash of water if needed to loosen. Taste and add more sugar if desired. Let cool to room temperature.
  3. Press Into Strands. Working in batches, press the cooled purée through a potato ricer directly over each serving dish, letting it fall into thin, vermicelli-like strands. (A coarse-holed colander works in a pinch.)
  4. Top and Chill. Spoon or pipe the whipped topping (or whipped cream for the dairy version) generously over the chestnut strands. Chill for at least 1 hour before serving.
  5. Serve. Garnish with shaved chocolate or a dusting of cocoa just before bringing to the table.
