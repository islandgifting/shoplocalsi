Dvar Torah · Parshas Ki Savo

The Basket That Teaches You How to Say Thank You

אֲרַמִּי אֹבֵד אָבִי — "An Aramean tried to destroy my father." Ki Savo opens with a farmer, a basket of first fruits, and a script of gratitude every Jew was required to say out loud — and it turns out that script is still teaching us how to thank Hashem today.

The Basket That Teaches You How to Say Thank You

אֲרַמִּי אֹבֵד אָבִי וַיֵּרֶד מִצְרַיְמָה וַיָּגָר שָם

"An Aramean tried to destroy my father, and he went down to Egypt and sojourned there..." (Devarim 26:5).

Ki Savo opens with the mitzvah of bikkurim: once Bnei Yisrael settle the Land, every farmer must bring a basket of his very first ripened fruits to the Beis Hamikdash and hand it to the Kohen — but the mitzvah doesn't end there. He has to stand and recite an entire declaration out loud, retelling the whole story from Yaakov's flight from Lavan, through the slavery in Mitzrayim, to the moment Hashem brought us into a land flowing with milk and honey. Gratitude, in the Torah's eyes, isn't just something you feel quietly. It's something you say.

🍅 Why Does Gratitude Need Words?

It would seem enough for the farmer to simply feel thankful as he hands over his basket. Yet the Torah insists on a spoken declaration — because feelings that stay unspoken have a way of fading, while a feeling put into words becomes something you can return to, teach your children, and build a life around. The Sforno notes that the farmer is required to remember specifically that we were once wanderers with nothing, so that the fruit in his hand never starts to feel like something he simply deserved.

💛 What It Looks Like at Home This Week

Later in this same parsha, the Torah warns that the curses befall us not because we stop serving Hashem, but because we do it "without joy and goodness of heart" (28:47) — even when we have everything. Bikkurim is the antidote: a built-in, spoken habit of noticing the good and saying so. This week, try making "thank you" a specific, spoken sentence at your table rather than a feeling left unsaid — naming exactly what you're grateful for, out loud, the way the Torah asked the farmer to do with his basket.

May we merit to notice our own baskets, and to say so. 🍅

Small tricks, big difference

Kitchen & Home Tips

Little hacks passed down from Bubbies, neighbors, and years of trial & error. Send us yours — we'll add them to the collection.

🌱
Elul is here

Stock up on honey before the rush

Rosh Hashana honey sells out fast once Elul is in full swing. Grab a jar or two now while the shelves are full and prices are normal.

🍱
Freezer trick

Double the soup, freeze half

Anytime you make soup, double the recipe and freeze half in a flat, labeled bag. It thaws faster and gives you an instant weeknight dinner later in Elul.

🌱
Produce hack

Keep herbs fresh for two weeks

Trim the stems and stand herbs in a cup of water like flowers, then loosely cover with a plastic bag and refrigerate. Parsley and cilantro stay crisp far longer than in their original packaging.

🧸
Cleaning hack

Sanitize your sponge in the microwave

Wet the sponge fully and microwave for one minute. It kills most of the bacteria hiding inside and makes the sponge smell fresh again — no need to toss it as often.

🥛
Pantry fix

Stop rice and salt from clumping

Drop a few grains of raw rice into your salt shaker or sugar canister. The rice absorbs excess moisture and keeps everything pouring freely, even on humid days.

🧲
Make-ahead

Freeze muffin batter, bake fresh

Portion unbaked muffin or coffee cake batter into a lined tin and freeze. Bake straight from frozen (add 5-8 extra minutes) for oven-fresh Shabbos morning treats.

🥚
Kitchen trick

Peel hard-boiled eggs in seconds

Shock eggs in an ice bath right after boiling, then gently roll each one on the counter to crackle the shell all over. It slips off in one or two pieces.

🔌
Shine trick

Polish silver candlesticks with toothpaste

A dab of plain white toothpaste on a soft cloth buffs tarnish off silver in minutes. Rinse well and dry before candle lighting for a real shine.

🍍
Rosh Hashana prep

Deseed a pomegranate in a bowl of water

Score the pomegranate and break it into sections underwater in a large bowl. The seeds sink to the bottom while the bitter white pith floats to the top — no stains on your clothes or counter, and cleanup is a breeze.

🥏
Baking hack

Warm honey pours smoother

Before measuring honey for your Rosh Hashana baking, warm the jar in a bowl of hot water for a few minutes or give it a quick zap in the microwave. It pours cleanly and you'll get every last drop out of the measuring cup.

🌽
Produce hack

Ripen hard avocados overnight

Tuck a hard avocado into a paper bag with a banana or apple and leave it on the counter overnight. The extra ethylene gas speeds up ripening so it's ready for salads or dips the next day.

For the little ones

Kids' Corner — No Electronics Needed

Ideas to save your sanity on long summer afternoons and hot Shabbos weekends. Grouped by situation — pick a tab, pick an idea, and go.

All ages

Memory tray challenge

Arrange 12 household items on a tray. Let everyone study it for one minute, then cover it up. Go around calling out items from memory — last one to forget wins.

Ages 5+

Bracha bee

Call out a food, kids race to say the correct bracha out loud. Get it right, you stay in; miss one, you sit out a round. Last one standing wins.

Ages 4+

Human knot

Everyone stands in a circle and grabs two different hands across the group. Working together (talking only, no letting go!) untangle back into one big circle.

Ages 3+

Balloon keep-it-up

Blow up a balloon before Shabbos starts. See how long the whole family can keep it from touching the floor — no hands allowed for the littlest ones is extra fun.

All ages

Backyard ice hunt

Freeze small toys in ice cube trays. Kids "excavate" them with spoons and warm water. Cools them down and buys you 45 minutes.

Ages 3+

Sidewalk chalk mural

Give each kid a section of the sidewalk. When done, walk the whole family through the "art gallery" and give awards.

Ages 5+

Lemonade stand

Set up out front. Real money, real customers. Kids learn making change, customer service, and marketing. Great mitzvah opportunity: donate to tzedakah.

All ages

The nature bingo card

Make a bingo card of things to find outside: red leaf, feather, three types of rocks, a bug, a puddle. Walk the block. First to bingo wins.

Ages 4+

Backyard obstacle course

Chairs, cushions, hula hoops, a broomstick. Have older kids design it. Time each other. Bonus: video the runs.

All ages

Sprinkler paintbrush

Give kids paintbrushes and buckets of water. Let them "paint" the fence, the driveway, the siding. Water evaporates — endless canvas.

Ages 4+

Sock puppet theater

Old socks + markers + buttons + yarn. Kids make puppets, then perform a show for the family after supper. Curtains: a blanket over two chairs.

Ages 3+

Restaurant at home

Kids write "menus," set the table, take orders. Serve real snacks. Amazing pretend-play. Great vocabulary building.

All ages

Fort under the dining table

Sheets, chairs, cushions, flashlights. Read books inside. Serve lunch in the fort as a special treat. Instant magic.

Ages 5+

Cardboard box marble maze

Cut open a cereal box or shoe box. Kids glue in cardboard pieces and straws to make a marble maze. Design + engineering + hours of play.

Ages 3+

Sensory bin

Big plastic tub + rice or dry beans + small toys hidden inside + scoops and cups. Kids can play for an hour. Contain to one room!

Ages 6+

Kid Iron Chef

Give kids 3 safe ingredients from the pantry. They "compete" to make a snack. You judge. Winner gets to pick supper for that night.

Ages 1-3

Muffin tin sort

Give a muffin tin and a bowl of pom-poms, cheerios, or small blocks. Toddler "sorts" by color into the tin. 30 quiet minutes.

Ages 2-4

Water pouring station

Two cups, one bowl of water, tray underneath. Toddler pours water back and forth. Improves fine motor. Bring towels.

Ages 1-3

Sticker page

Sheet of stickers + blank paper. Toddlers can peel and stick for a shockingly long time. Buy stickers in bulk on Amazon.

Ages 2+

Play-dough tray

Homemade play-dough (flour, salt, water, food color) + cookie cutters + a rolling pin. Set them up at the table. Save the tray for next time.

Ages 1-2

Basket of "safe" kitchen tools

Wooden spoons, plastic measuring cups, silicone spatulas, small pot. Give them their own drawer in the kitchen. Endless fascination.

Ages 2-4

Painter's tape road map

Painter's tape on the floor becomes roads. Toy cars zoom around. Easy to peel up after. Endless configurations.

This week's portion, in brief

Parsha Short — Ki Savo

Devarim 26:1 – 29:8

A Basket, a Declaration, and a Blessing

Ki Savo opens with bikkurim — the mitzvah to bring your very first ripened fruits to the Beis Hamikdash and recite a full declaration of gratitude, retracing our story from Yaakov's flight from Lavan through the Exodus to the Land itself. From there the parsha moves into the mitzvah of tithing, then a public ceremony on Har Gerizim and Har Eival where the entire nation affirms its commitment to the Torah.

The bulk of the parsha is the Tochacha — the lengthy, sobering list of blessings for following Hashem's ways and curses for straying from them. Chazal note something striking: the Torah attributes the curses not to abandoning the mitzvos, but to serving Hashem "without joy and goodness of heart" — even in a time of abundance.

וְמָלנו אֶת ה' אֱלֹהֶיךָ הַיּוֹם לַמיב מֵיטִיבDevarim 26:16
For the Shabbos Table

The Root of Unhappiness

תַחַת אֲשֶּר לַא עָבַדְתָ אֶת ה' אֱלֹהֶיךָ בְשִמְחָה וֹבְטוֹב לֵבָב

"Because you did not serve Hashem your G-d with happiness and goodness of heart, when you had everything in abundance" (Devarim 28:47). Rabbi Yochanan Zweig points out that this pasuk describes people who had everything and were still unhappy — a very contemporary picture. A person who feels entitled to what he has can never simply enjoy it; he's too busy resenting whatever he still doesn't have.

A short thought to share at the table: true happiness doesn't come from getting more, it comes from appreciating what's already in front of you. This week, try naming one thing at the table you're genuinely grateful for — out loud, the way the Torah asked the bikkurim farmer to do with his basket.

Halachos of Shabbos

Timely this year: Rosh Hashana 5787 falls on Shabbos

Why the Shofar Will Be Silent on Day One

כֵּיוָן כֵּל חַיָּב בִתְקִיעַת שוֹפָר וְאֵין הַכֹּל בֵּמֶן לִתְקֹע

This year Rosh Hashana falls Friday night through Sunday night, which means the first day — Shabbos — will pass without the sound of the shofar at all; it's blown only on the second day. The Gemara (Rosh Hashana 29b) records that min haTorah, blowing shofar on Shabbos is entirely permitted. It was Chazal who instituted a gezeirah forbidding it, out of concern that someone not yet skilled in blowing might carry the shofar through a public domain on the way to a teacher to learn — forgetting, in the moment, that carrying is itself forbidden on Shabbos.

Interestingly, this decree wasn't universal even in Talmudic times: in the Beis Hamikdash itself the shofar was sounded on Shabbos, and after the Churban, Rabban Yochanan ben Zakkai instituted that any place with a functioning beis din of twenty-three judges could continue the practice — a leniency the Gemara says was enacted specifically in Yavneh. Today, the accepted practice in every community is that the shofar is not blown when Rosh Hashana falls on Shabbos; instead, we rely on hearing it the following day.

Sourced from Rosh Hashana 29b and the Shulchan Aruch (Orach Chaim 588). This is a general overview for learning purposes — for any specific situation, please ask your own Rav.

A weekly series

39 Melachos — Week 4: Zoreh

The Melacha of Winnowing

Zoreh: Separating With the Force of Wind

Zoreh follows right after Dosh in the Torah's process of making bread: once the grain was threshed, it was tossed into the air so the wind could carry off the light chaff while the heavier kernels fell back down. The defining feature of this melacha is using the force of wind — natural or artificial — to separate something unwanted from a food or mixture. That's why blowing on a mix of peanuts and their thin papery shells to scatter the shells away is also a form of Zoreh, even though no actual wind is involved.

This shows up in a few everyday moments most of us wouldn't expect. The Rama, citing a Yerushalmi, writes that one should be careful not to blow against the wind in a way that scatters saliva into mist. Poskim likewise caution against shaking crumbs out of a tablecloth into the wind, or hanging musty garments and seforim outside specifically to let the wind air them out — both cross into the same territory of using wind to separate unwanted matter away.

On the more permissive side, aerosol sprays are not a violation of Zoreh, so perfume or deodorant may be sprayed on the body (though not directly onto clothing). Rav Moshe Feinstein and others permit using a sink's spray faucet attachment on Shabbos, provided only cold water is used (hot water raises separate concerns of Bishul). Adding cold water to a vaporizer that's already running from before Shabbos is likewise permitted when there's a genuine need.

Woman of Valor

This week's reflection

Chana — The Woman Whose Prayer Rewrote How We Daven

Chana, wife of Elkanah, was childless for years while her household's other wife bore children and taunted her about it — and yet the Navi tells us she brought her pain straight to Hashem rather than letting it curdle into bitterness. Every year the family traveled to the Mishkan in Shiloh, and every year Chana wept and prayed to be remembered with a child of her own.

It's what she did while praying that changed Klal Yisrael forever. Chana stood and moved her lips in silent, heartfelt tefillah — so unlike the audible communal prayer of her day that Eli the Kohen Gadol assumed she was drunk. When she explained she was simply pouring out her heart to Hashem, the Gemara in Berachos derives from her the basic halachos of the Amidah: that we pray silently, that our lips must move, that we may not raise our voice. Chazal teach that Chana was answered on Rosh Hashana itself, together with Sarah and Rachel — which is why her story is read as the haftorah on the first day of the Yamim Noraim, a living lesson that sincere, quiet prayer can pierce the Heavens.

In honor of Chana Imeinu · mother of Shmuel HaNavi, whose tefillah still shapes how we daven — and of every Staten Island woman pouring out her heart this Elul
A thought to carry with us

This Week's Chinuch Nugget

This week

Say the Thank You Out Loud

The farmer bringing bikkurim doesn't just feel grateful — he's required to say an entire declaration out loud, naming exactly what Hashem did for him. Kids absorb gratitude the same way: a quiet feeling barely registers, but hearing a parent name something specific ("I'm so thankful you helped clear the table without being asked") teaches them what gratitude actually sounds like. This week, try replacing a general "thank you" with one specific, spoken sentence at least once a day.
Parenting Wisdom · Week of Parshas Ki Savo
A second thought

Serve With a Smile, Not Just Compliance

The Tochacha's curses are blamed not on disobedience, but on serving Hashem "without joy" — even surrounded by abundance. Kids notice the difference between a parent who does chesed or keeps Shabbos with a sigh and one who does it with a smile, and they absorb which one they want to imitate. As Elul winds down, it's worth asking ourselves: are the mitzvos in our home landing as a joy or a burden? Our tone teaches louder than our words.
