Dvar Torah · Parshas Ki Teitzei

The Mother Bird You're Not Supposed to See

כִּי יִקָּרֵא קַן צִפּוֹר לְפָנֶיךָ — "If a bird's nest happens to be before you." Of all the mitzvos in a parsha packed with 74 of them, this quiet one still stops people in their tracks.

The Mother Bird You're Not Supposed to See

כִּי יִקָּרֵא קַן צִפּוֹר לְפָנֶיךָ... שַלֵּחַ תְשַלַּח אֶת הָאֵם וְאֶת הַבָנִים תִּקַּח לָךְ

"If a bird's nest happens to be before you on the way... you shall surely send away the mother, and take the young for yourself" (Devarim 22:6–7).

Ki Teitzei has more mitzvos in it than any other parsha in the Torah — the Rambam counts 74. Tucked into the middle of laws about war, marriage, and business ethics is this small, specific scene: you find a nest, mother bird sitting on eggs or chicks. If you want to take them, you may — but first, you have to send the mother away so she doesn't have to watch.

🕊 Why Send Her Away First?

The Ramban explains that this mitzvah exists to train our own middos — to build the muscle of compassion in us, even toward a creature that will barely notice the difference. The Gemara in Berachos, meanwhile, is careful to note that we don't say this mitzvah exists because Hashem has special pity on birds specifically — the deeper point is a chok, a Divine decree, that happens to shape our sensitivity along the way. Both ideas can be true together: we may not always know the ultimate "why," but the mitzvah still refines how we treat every living thing in front of us.

🏫 What It Looks Like at Home This Week

Kids are heading back into classrooms this week — new teachers, new seating charts, new social dynamics. Shiluach hakan is a quiet reminder that even when something difficult or necessary has to happen, we're still responsible for how it happens and who's watching. Correcting a child doesn't have to happen in front of the whole carpool line. A hard conversation with a friend doesn't need an audience. Even routine, permitted moments deserve a measure of dignity for everyone involved.

May we merit to notice the small moments where compassion is asked of us. 🕊

Small tricks, big difference

Kitchen & Home Tips

Little hacks passed down from Bubbies, neighbors, and years of trial & error. Send us yours — we'll add them to the collection.

🌱
Elul is here

Stock up on honey before the rush

Rosh Hashana honey sells out fast once Elul is in full swing. Grab a jar or two now while the shelves are full and prices are normal.

🍱
Freezer trick

Double the soup, freeze half

Anytime you make soup, double the recipe and freeze half in a flat, labeled bag. It thaws faster and gives you an instant weeknight dinner later in Elul.

🌱
Produce hack

Keep herbs fresh for two weeks

Trim the stems and stand herbs in a cup of water like flowers, then loosely cover with a plastic bag and refrigerate. Parsley and cilantro stay crisp far longer than in their original packaging.

🧸
Cleaning hack

Sanitize your sponge in the microwave

Wet the sponge fully and microwave for one minute. It kills most of the bacteria hiding inside and makes the sponge smell fresh again — no need to toss it as often.

🥛
Pantry fix

Stop rice and salt from clumping

Drop a few grains of raw rice into your salt shaker or sugar canister. The rice absorbs excess moisture and keeps everything pouring freely, even on humid days.

🧲
Make-ahead

Freeze muffin batter, bake fresh

Portion unbaked muffin or coffee cake batter into a lined tin and freeze. Bake straight from frozen (add 5-8 extra minutes) for oven-fresh Shabbos morning treats.

🥚
Kitchen trick

Peel hard-boiled eggs in seconds

Shock eggs in an ice bath right after boiling, then gently roll each one on the counter to crackle the shell all over. It slips off in one or two pieces.

🔌
Shine trick

Polish silver candlesticks with toothpaste

A dab of plain white toothpaste on a soft cloth buffs tarnish off silver in minutes. Rinse well and dry before candle lighting for a real shine.

For the little ones

Kids' Corner — No Electronics Needed

Ideas to save your sanity on long summer afternoons and hot Shabbos weekends. Grouped by situation — pick a tab, pick an idea, and go.

All ages

Memory tray challenge

Arrange 12 household items on a tray. Let everyone study it for one minute, then cover it up. Go around calling out items from memory — last one to forget wins.

Ages 5+

Bracha bee

Call out a food, kids race to say the correct bracha out loud. Get it right, you stay in; miss one, you sit out a round. Last one standing wins.

Ages 4+

Human knot

Everyone stands in a circle and grabs two different hands across the group. Working together (talking only, no letting go!) untangle back into one big circle.

Ages 3+

Balloon keep-it-up

Blow up a balloon before Shabbos starts. See how long the whole family can keep it from touching the floor — no hands allowed for the littlest ones is extra fun.

All ages

Backyard ice hunt

Freeze small toys in ice cube trays. Kids "excavate" them with spoons and warm water. Cools them down and buys you 45 minutes.

Ages 3+

Sidewalk chalk mural

Give each kid a section of the sidewalk. When done, walk the whole family through the "art gallery" and give awards.

Ages 5+

Lemonade stand

Set up out front. Real money, real customers. Kids learn making change, customer service, and marketing. Great mitzvah opportunity: donate to tzedakah.

All ages

The nature bingo card

Make a bingo card of things to find outside: red leaf, feather, three types of rocks, a bug, a puddle. Walk the block. First to bingo wins.

Ages 4+

Backyard obstacle course

Chairs, cushions, hula hoops, a broomstick. Have older kids design it. Time each other. Bonus: video the runs.

All ages

Sprinkler paintbrush

Give kids paintbrushes and buckets of water. Let them "paint" the fence, the driveway, the siding. Water evaporates — endless canvas.

Ages 4+

Sock puppet theater

Old socks + markers + buttons + yarn. Kids make puppets, then perform a show for the family after supper. Curtains: a blanket over two chairs.

Ages 3+

Restaurant at home

Kids write "menus," set the table, take orders. Serve real snacks. Amazing pretend-play. Great vocabulary building.

All ages

Fort under the dining table

Sheets, chairs, cushions, flashlights. Read books inside. Serve lunch in the fort as a special treat. Instant magic.

Ages 5+

Cardboard box marble maze

Cut open a cereal box or shoe box. Kids glue in cardboard pieces and straws to make a marble maze. Design + engineering + hours of play.

Ages 3+

Sensory bin

Big plastic tub + rice or dry beans + small toys hidden inside + scoops and cups. Kids can play for an hour. Contain to one room!

Ages 6+

Kid Iron Chef

Give kids 3 safe ingredients from the pantry. They "compete" to make a snack. You judge. Winner gets to pick supper for that night.

Ages 1-3

Muffin tin sort

Give a muffin tin and a bowl of pom-poms, cheerios, or small blocks. Toddler "sorts" by color into the tin. 30 quiet minutes.

Ages 2-4

Water pouring station

Two cups, one bowl of water, tray underneath. Toddler pours water back and forth. Improves fine motor. Bring towels.

Ages 1-3

Sticker page

Sheet of stickers + blank paper. Toddlers can peel and stick for a shockingly long time. Buy stickers in bulk on Amazon.

Ages 2+

Play-dough tray

Homemade play-dough (flour, salt, water, food color) + cookie cutters + a rolling pin. Set them up at the table. Save the tray for next time.

Ages 1-2

Basket of "safe" kitchen tools

Wooden spoons, plastic measuring cups, silicone spatulas, small pot. Give them their own drawer in the kitchen. Endless fascination.

Ages 2-4

Painter's tape road map

Painter's tape on the floor becomes roads. Toy cars zoom around. Easy to peel up after. Endless configurations.

This week's portion, in brief

Parsha Short — Ki Teitzei

Devarim 21:10 – 25:19

More Mitzvos Than Any Other Parsha

Ki Teitzei is famous for one thing above all: volume. The Rambam counts 74 mitzvos packed into these five perakim — more than any other parsha in the Torah. It covers laws about going out to war, family and marriage, an employer's obligations to workers, forbidden mixtures, tzitzis, and shiluach hakan (sending away a mother bird before taking her young).

Running through the middle of it all is a repeated theme: don't look away. The parsha commands returning a neighbor's lost animal, helping unload a struggling animal on the road, and paying a hired worker on time — each one a small, practical refusal to pretend you didn't notice.

The parsha closes with the command to remember what Amalek did on the way out of Mitzrayim, and to blot out their memory — a stark final note after so many chapters about how to treat the vulnerable with care.

לֹא תִרְאֶה אֶת שֹוֹר אָחִיךָ אוֹ אֶת שֶּיוֹ נִדָּחִים וְהִתְעַלַּמְּתָּ מֵהֶםDevarim 22:1
For the Shabbos Table

Lo Suchal L'hisalem — You May Not Hide Yourself

לֹא תִרְאֶה אֶת שֹוֹר אָחִיךָ אוֹ אֶת שֶּיוֹ נִדָּחִים וְהִתְעַלַּמְּתָּ מֵהֶם... הָשֵּב תְשִיבֵם לְאָחִיךָ

"You shall not see your brother's ox or sheep wandering and hide yourself from them; you shall surely return them to your brother" (Devarim 22:1). The word the Torah uses for looking away — hisalem, from the same root as "to hide" — is the real chiddush here. It's not enough to simply avoid doing harm; the Torah forbids the more passive sin of turning your face away when you could have helped.

A short thought to share at the table: how many times a week do we quietly decide something "isn't our problem" — a classmate sitting alone, a neighbor struggling with bags, a friend who seems a little off? Ki Teitzei's answer is that noticing is itself an obligation, not an option.

Halachos of Shabbos

Through Shmini Atzeres

Why We Add L'Dovid Hashem Ori This Time of Year

לְדָוִד ה' אוֹרִי וְיִשְעִי

From Rosh Chodesh Elul until Shmini Atzeres, many have the custom to add Perek 27 of Tehillim — "L'Dovid Hashem Ori v'Yishi" — after Shacharis and Mincha (some communities add it at Maariv as well). Since this stretch of the year includes plenty of Shabbosos, that means this perek becomes part of your Shabbos davening too, not just the weekdays.

The Sefer Elef L'Magen explains the custom beautifully: this perek alludes directly to the Yamim Noraim and Sukkos. "Ori" (my light) refers to Rosh Hashanah, "Yishi" (my salvation) to Yom Kippur, and "v'yitzpeneni b'sukoh" (He will shelter me in His sukkah) to Sukkos — so it's fitting to recite it precisely during this season. The Divrei Malkiel adds that this perek alludes to the future day of judgment, when the nations will bring accusations against Klal Yisrael, and reciting L'Dovid offers protection against those claims — which is why many say it specifically at Shacharis (as the day's judgment begins) and Mincha (as it concludes), while those who add it at Maariv are following the "din" of Yerushalayim time.

Sourced from the Young Israel of Staten Island's weekly halacha feature (Sefer Elef L'Magen; Divrei Malkiel). This is a general overview for learning purposes — for any specific situation, please ask your own Rav.

39 Melachos — Week 3: Dosh

The Melacha of Threshing

Dosh: Separating Food From Its Natural Growth

Dosh means removing a food from the natural covering or attachment it grew in — the classic example is threshing, separating seeds from the stalks and husks they grew inside. In the Mishkan, Dosh was used to thresh seeds so their coloring could be extracted for dyes. A toldah (secondary form) of Dosh is mefarek — squeezing: biblically, this applies to squeezing grapes for their juice or olives for their oil, and by extension, squeezing liquid out of a wet cloth.

Practically, this touches a surprising number of kitchen and bathroom moments. Shelling nuts like pecans, walnuts (the inner hard shell), or peanuts is permitted — but removing the thick outer pulpy hull of an almond or walnut is not, since that hull is considered part of the food's natural covering. Squeezing a fruit specifically to extract its juice (like a lemon into an empty cup) touches on real questions of Dosh and is best avoided; squeezing a bit of lemon directly onto food you're about to eat is more widely permitted.

Wringing out a wet towel, sponge, or washcloth is squarely forbidden as mefarek — the liquid is being separated from the cloth it's absorbed into. This is why many families keep a designated "Shabbos sponge" that's used damp but never wrung, and towels are patted rather than twisted dry.

Sourced from Halachipedia (citing the Mishna Berurah and Shemiras Shabbos Kehilchasa) and contemporary poskim. This is a general overview for learning purposes — for any specific situation, please ask your own Rav.

Woman of Valor

This week's reflection

Sarah Schenirer — The Seamstress Who Built a School System

Sarah Schenirer was born in Krakow in 1883 and, like most Orthodox girls of her generation, received almost no formal Jewish education — while her brothers learned in cheder, she was sent to a Polish public school and later became a dressmaker to help support her family. It bothered her deeply that so many bright, sincere young women around her were drifting from observance simply because no one had ever taught them why their Yiddishkeit mattered.

In 1917, working out of a small rented room in Krakow, she opened a class for 25 girls — the very first Bais Yaakov. She had no formal training as an educator and no institutional backing; she had conviction, and she kept building. By the time she passed away in 1935 at just 51, that single classroom had grown into a network of hundreds of schools stretching across Poland, complete with teacher seminaries and youth programs — the foundation of the Orthodox girls' education movement that shapes classrooms, including right here on Staten Island, to this very day.

In honor of Sarah Schenirer z"l · 1883–1935, founder of the Bais Yaakov movement — and of every Staten Island teacher walking back into her classroom this week
A thought to carry with us

This Week's Chinuch Nugget

This week

Don't Look Away

Ki Teitzei's laws of returning a lost object open with the words "lo suchal l'hisalem" — you may not hide yourself. Kids hear that and picture a lost wallet or a stray bike, but the deeper lesson is about people. As everyone settles back into new classrooms and new seating charts this week, try asking at dinner: "Did you notice anyone today who looked like they needed a friend?" It teaches that looking away is a choice — and that Torah asks us to choose to see.
