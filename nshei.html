Dvar Torah · Parshas Nitzavim-Vayeilech

Everyone Is Standing — No Exceptions

אַתֶּם נִצָבִים הַיּוֹם — "You are standing today, all of you." Nitzavim opens with the entire nation lined up together — leaders and woodchoppers, elders and children — entering the covenant as one body. Days before Rosh Hashana, the Torah is handing us the one idea we most need walking into judgment: nobody stands outside the circle.

Everyone Is Standing — No Exceptions

אַתֶּם נִצָבִים הַיּוֹם כֻּלְכֶם לִפְנֵי ה' אֱלֹהֵיכֶם

"You are standing today, all of you, before Hashem your G-d" (Devarim 29:9).

Nitzavim opens with Moshe gathering the entire nation — and the pasuk goes out of its way to list who's included: heads of tribes, elders, officers, "every man of Israel," children, wives, and even "the woodchopper and the water-drawer." Every rung of the ladder, from the most prominent to the most overlooked, stands in the same covenant at the same moment. Rashi notes that Moshe assembled them specifically on the day of his death, so that the closing of one era would be marked by the strongest possible statement: this Torah belongs to everybody, together.

🌟 Lo Bashamayim Hi — Torah Isn't Out of Reach

A few pesukim later comes one of the most comforting lines in the entire Torah: teshuva "is not in the heavens, that you should say, who will go up to the heavens and get it for us... it is very near to you, in your mouth and in your heart, to do it" (30:12–14). Coming back to Hashem was never meant to require a scholar's ladder or a tzaddik's climb. It's already sitting inside every single person named in that opening pasuk — the officer and the water-drawer both have equal access.

🧍 What It Looks Like at Home This Week

Vayeilech closes out the Torah reading with the mitzvah of Hakhel — men, women, and children gathered together to hear the king read the Torah aloud, so that "those who do not know may hear and learn." The pattern repeats: nobody is too young, too busy, or too far along to belong in the room. As Rosh Hashana approaches, it's worth asking who in our own homes might be quietly feeling like the woodchopper at the back of the line — and finding one small way this week to make sure they know they're standing right up front with everyone else.

May we all merit to stand together, ready for a good and sweet new year. 🧍

Small tricks, big difference

Kitchen & Home Tips

Little hacks passed down from Bubbies, neighbors, and years of trial & error. Send us yours — we'll add them to the collection.

🍍
This week's featured tip

Deseed a Pomegranate in a Bowl of Water — No Stains, No Mess

Score the pomegranate around the middle and gently break it into sections while fully submerged in a large bowl of water. The seeds sink straight to the bottom, the bitter white pith floats up to the top so you can just skim it off, and not a single drop of juice touches your clothing or countertop. With pomegranates showing up on so many Rosh Hashana tables this year, this is the trick worth actually remembering — deseed the whole fruit in the sink in under five minutes.

🍌
Rosh Hashana prep

Freeze pomegranate seeds for later

Once you've deseeded a pomegranate in water (see above), spread the seeds on a paper-towel-lined tray and freeze for an hour before bagging. They stay separate instead of clumping, so you can grab a handful anytime for salads or the Yom Tov table.

🍌
Elul is here

Stock up on honey before the rush

Rosh Hashana honey sells out fast once Elul is in full swing. Grab a jar or two now while the shelves are full and prices are normal.

🌱
Back to school

Pre-pack a week of school lunches

On a slow Sunday, assemble five lunch bags' worth of non-perishables (crackers, pretzels, fruit snacks) into labeled bins in the pantry. Mornings go faster when only the sandwich and fruit are left to grab.

🧸
Cleaning hack

Sanitize your sponge in the microwave

Wet the sponge fully and microwave for one minute. It kills most of the bacteria hiding inside and makes the sponge smell fresh again — no need to toss it as often.

🍎
Produce hack

Keep apples crisp for weeks

Store apples in the crisper drawer wrapped loosely in a paper towel rather than piled loose in a bowl. They ripen slower and stay crunchy right through the Yom Tov season.

🧲
Make-ahead

Freeze muffin batter, bake fresh

Portion unbaked muffin or coffee cake batter into a lined tin and freeze. Bake straight from frozen (add 5-8 extra minutes) for oven-fresh Shabbos morning treats.

🔌
Shine trick

Polish silver candlesticks with toothpaste

A dab of plain white toothpaste on a soft cloth buffs tarnish off silver in minutes. Rinse well and dry before candle lighting for a real shine.

🥏
Baking hack

Warm honey pours smoother

Before measuring honey for your Rosh Hashana baking, warm the jar in a bowl of hot water for a few minutes or give it a quick zap in the microwave. It pours cleanly and you'll get every last drop out of the measuring cup.

🌽
Produce hack

Ripen hard avocados overnight

Tuck a hard avocado into a paper bag with a banana or apple and leave it on the counter overnight. The extra ethylene gas speeds up ripening so it's ready for salads or dips the next day.

📦
Organizing hack

Label Yom Tov dishes before you pack them away

As you unpack the Rosh Hashana dishes this year, stick a small piece of painter's tape on each box listing exactly what's inside. Next year's unpacking (and next year's packing away) takes half the time.

For the little ones

Kids' Corner — No Electronics Needed

Ideas to save your sanity on long summer afternoons and hot Shabbos weekends. Grouped by situation — pick a tab, pick an idea, and go.

All ages

Memory tray challenge

Arrange 12 household items on a tray. Let everyone study it for one minute, then cover it up. Go around calling out items from memory — last one to forget wins.

Ages 5+

Bracha bee

Call out a food, kids race to say the correct bracha out loud. Get it right, you stay in; miss one, you sit out a round. Last one standing wins.

Ages 4+

Human knot

Everyone stands in a circle and grabs two different hands across the group. Working together (talking only, no letting go!) untangle back into one big circle.

Ages 3+

Balloon keep-it-up

Blow up a balloon before Shabbos starts. See how long the whole family can keep it from touching the floor — no hands allowed for the littlest ones is extra fun.

All ages

Backyard ice hunt

Freeze small toys in ice cube trays. Kids "excavate" them with spoons and warm water. Cools them down and buys you 45 minutes.

Ages 3+

Sidewalk chalk mural

Give each kid a section of the sidewalk. When done, walk the whole family through the "art gallery" and give awards.

Ages 5+

Lemonade stand

Set up out front. Real money, real customers. Kids learn making change, customer service, and marketing. Great mitzvah opportunity: donate to tzedakah.

All ages

The nature bingo card

Make a bingo card of things to find outside: red leaf, feather, three types of rocks, a bug, a puddle. Walk the block. First to bingo wins.

Ages 4+

Backyard obstacle course

Chairs, cushions, hula hoops, a broomstick. Have older kids design it. Time each other. Bonus: video the runs.

All ages

Sprinkler paintbrush

Give kids paintbrushes and buckets of water. Let them "paint" the fence, the driveway, the siding. Water evaporates — endless canvas.

Ages 4+

Sock puppet theater

Old socks + markers + buttons + yarn. Kids make puppets, then perform a show for the family after supper. Curtains: a blanket over two chairs.

Ages 3+

Restaurant at home

Kids write "menus," set the table, take orders. Serve real snacks. Amazing pretend-play. Great vocabulary building.

All ages

Fort under the dining table

Sheets, chairs, cushions, flashlights. Read books inside. Serve lunch in the fort as a special treat. Instant magic.

Ages 5+

Cardboard box marble maze

Cut open a cereal box or shoe box. Kids glue in cardboard pieces and straws to make a marble maze. Design + engineering + hours of play.

Ages 3+

Sensory bin

Big plastic tub + rice or dry beans + small toys hidden inside + scoops and cups. Kids can play for an hour. Contain to one room!

Ages 6+

Kid Iron Chef

Give kids 3 safe ingredients from the pantry. They "compete" to make a snack. You judge. Winner gets to pick supper for that night.

Ages 1-3

Muffin tin sort

Give a muffin tin and a bowl of pom-poms, cheerios, or small blocks. Toddler "sorts" by color into the tin. 30 quiet minutes.

Ages 2-4

Water pouring station

Two cups, one bowl of water, tray underneath. Toddler pours water back and forth. Improves fine motor. Bring towels.

Ages 1-3

Sticker page

Sheet of stickers + blank paper. Toddlers can peel and stick for a shockingly long time. Buy stickers in bulk on Amazon.

Ages 2+

Play-dough tray

Homemade play-dough (flour, salt, water, food color) + cookie cutters + a rolling pin. Set them up at the table. Save the tray for next time.

Ages 1-2

Basket of "safe" kitchen tools

Wooden spoons, plastic measuring cups, silicone spatulas, small pot. Give them their own drawer in the kitchen. Endless fascination.

Ages 2-4

Painter's tape road map

Painter's tape on the floor becomes roads. Toy cars zoom around. Easy to peel up after. Endless configurations.

This week's portion, in brief

Parsha Short — Nitzavim-Vayeilech

Devarim 29:9 – 31:30

A Covenant for Every Single Person, and a Torah Within Reach

Nitzavim opens with the entire nation — from tribal heads down to the woodchoppers and water-drawers — standing together to enter the covenant as one body, including generations not yet born. Moshe then reassures them that if they ever stray and return, teshuva is "not in the heavens" and "not across the sea" — it's already close, "in your mouth and in your heart."

Vayeilech carries the story into Moshe's final day: he hands leadership to Yehoshua, writes the final Torah scroll, and commands the mitzvah of Hakhel — gathering the whole nation, men, women, and children, every seven years to hear the Torah read aloud, so that even those who don't yet know it can hear and learn.

כִּי קָרוֹב אֵלֶיךָ הַּדָבָר מְאֹדDevarim 30:14
For the Shabbos Table

You Don't Need a Ladder to the Heavens

לֹא בַּשָּמַיִם הִוא לֵאֹמֹר מִי יַעֲלֶה לָנוּ הַּשָּמַיְמָה

"It is not in the heavens, that you should say, who will go up to the heavens and bring it to us" (Devarim 30:12). Moshe is speaking here about teshuva right before Rosh Hashana, and the message is meant to feel personal to every single person, not just the scholars: coming back to Hashem was never designed to require some impossible climb. It's already inside you — "in your mouth and in your heart, to do it."

A short thought to share at the table: nobody at the covenant in Nitzavim was too small to count, from the tribal leader down to the water-drawer. This week, going into Rosh Hashana, it's worth remembering that teshuva doesn't ask us to become someone else — it asks us to come home to who we already are, one honest step at a time.

Halachos of Shabbos

Getting ready for Yom Tov meals eaten straight through Shabbos

Chazarah: Returning Food to the Fire on Shabbos

אֵין מחִזִין אֶלָּ אַּלָּ אִם כִּן מן הַגָּּרוֹם כּן מוֹמֶֿ לֹא

With Rosh Hashana leading straight into Shabbos this year, plenty of us will be reheating soup, kugel, or chicken for a second or third meal — which makes this a good week to review the halachos of chazarah, returning food to a flame or blech on Shabbos. The Shulchan Aruch (Orach Chaim 253:2) sets out the classic conditions: the fire must have a blech or other heat-diminishing covering (a hekesh), the pot must not yet have been set down on the ground (i.e. it stayed in your hand or on a counter the whole time), you must still intend to eat from it again, and the food must still be considered "warm" — meaning it never cooled down to room temperature.

The Mishna Brurah adds a further well-known stringency: chazarah is only for a pot that is fully cooked already (chason gamur). Placing a raw or undercooked food onto the blech for the first time is a Torah-level violation of Bishul, not a question of chazarah at all. Practically, this means the Yom Tov soup pot that's been simmering since before candle lighting may be moved back onto the blech after ladling out a bowl, but a pot of food that never touched the fire before Shabbos may not be placed there for the first time, however warm it may already be.

Shemiras Shabbos Kehilchasa (ch. 1) also discusses the common case of an electric hotplate or urn: since it typically has no adjustable dial (or the dial is covered before Shabbos), it functions like a blech for these purposes, and the same four conditions apply. A pot that was fully removed from the blech and placed on the table or counter loses its chazarah status the moment it's set down — from that point, returning it needs its own fresh review.

Sourced from Shulchan Aruch Orach Chaim 253, Mishna Brurah ad loc., and Shemiras Shabbos Kehilchasa. This is a general overview for learning purposes — for any specific situation, please ask your own Rav.

39 Melachos — Week 5: Borer

The Melacha of Selecting/Sorting

Borer: Separating Good From Bad

Borer comes right after Zoreh in the Torah's bread-making sequence: once the chaff blew away, the kernels still needed to be picked over by hand to remove small stones and debris before grinding. The defining feature of this melacha is separating a mixture of two or more different items so that one type is set apart from the rest — something we do constantly at a table full of guests, salads, and mixed leftovers.

There are three ways to violate Borer on a Torah level, and Chazal established one set of conditions that permits sorting anyway. The permitted way to separate requires all three at once: you take the food you want to eat (ochel) away from the waste (pesolet), not the other way around; you separate by hand, not with a tool like a strainer or slotted spoon designed for sorting; and you do it toward immediate use, meaning you're eating or serving it right away rather than sorting now for later. Miss any one of the three and it becomes a problem — for example, using a strainer to separate pasta from its cooking water is generally permitted since pesolet (water) is being drained through a designed opening rather than a food item being lifted out, but poskim discuss this case carefully since it can look like sorting through a tool.

Familiar Yom Tov examples: peeling a banana, orange, egg, onion, or potato is not Borer, since the peel was never edible mixed in with the fruit — it's considered its own separate layer, so peeling and immediately eating is fine. Picking the good pieces of fish out of a can while leaving bones behind, or removing seeds from a cut melon or cantaloupe, follows the same three-part rule: by hand, food from waste, for immediate use. Setting out a bowl of mixed nuts and picking out only the almonds to eat now is permitted; picking out the almonds now to save for tomorrow's kugel is not.

Woman of Valor

This week's reflection

Rebbetzin Esther Jungreis — The Rebbetzin Who Rebuilt From Ashes

Esther Jungreis survived Bergen-Belsen as a child, watching the world she knew be destroyed — and yet she emerged not broken but determined that Am Yisrael's story would not end there. Her father, the Nitra Rav, had told her while still in the camp: "We will rebuild. Am Yisrael Chai." She carried that sentence with her for the rest of her life, and eventually built an entire movement out of it.

In 1973, at a moment when so many Jews were drifting from Torah observance, she stood up in Madison Square Garden and filled it — the first of countless "Hineni" gatherings that brought tens of thousands of unaffiliated Jews back to their heritage, one warm, unapologetic talk at a time. She never spoke down to anyone; she spoke as someone who had lost almost everything and still chose, with total conviction, to love being Jewish. Rebbetzin Jungreis went on to found the Hineni Heritage Center, write bestselling books, and mentor a generation of women toward a Yiddishkeit rooted not in guilt but in joy and dignity — a living answer, decades later, to her father's promise in the camp.

In honor of Rebbetzin Esther Jungreis a"h · founder of Hineni, who turned survival into a lifetime of kiruv — and of every Staten Island woman carrying that same fire into 5787
A thought to carry with us

This Week's Chinuch Nugget

This week

Nobody Stands at the Back of the Line

Nitzavim opens by naming everyone at the covenant — tribal heads down to the woodchopper and water-drawer — making sure no one feels like an afterthought. Kids pick up fast on who gets top billing in a family and who feels like an extra. This week, try making a point of asking your quietest or youngest child their opinion first at the table, the way the Torah made sure the "smallest" person in the nation was named right alongside the leaders.
Parenting Wisdom · Week of Parshas Nitzavim-Vayeilech
A second thought

Mistakes Aren't Out of Reach to Fix

"It is not in the heavens... it is very near to you, in your mouth and in your heart, to do it." Moshe tells the whole nation that coming back from a mistake was never meant to feel impossible. Kids who believe a mistake makes them "bad" freeze up instead of trying again; kids who believe fixing it is simple and close by actually do it. This week, when a child messes up, try saying "that's an easy one to fix" instead of dwelling on the mistake itself — and watch how much faster they move toward making it right.
