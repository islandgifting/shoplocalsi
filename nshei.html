Dvar Torah · Shabbos Chol Hamoed Sukkos

The Home We Choose to Leave

Right after building a Mishkan sturdy enough to house the Shechinah, Klal Yisrael's very next building project was a hut with a roof you can see the stars through. Sukkos asks us to trade our solid, locked, climate-controlled homes for a few days in a structure that could blow over in a strong wind — and somehow that's when we're commanded to feel our deepest joy of the entire year.

The Home We Choose to Leave

כִּי בֶַּּימֵַי עדוֹט וְבימֵי רִֶבֵז יֵשיבֻ בֶּנֵי יִשְרָאֵל בֶַּּּכֹת

"For seven days you shall dwell in booths... so that your generations will know that I caused Bnei Yisrael to dwell in booths when I took them out of the land of Egypt" (Vayikra 23:42–43).

Timing matters here. We don't build our sukkah in the spring, to mark leaving Mitzrayim itself — we build it now, in the fall, right after Yom Kippur. Moshe came down from Har Sinai on Yom Kippur with the second luchos and Hashem's forgiveness in hand, and almost immediately Klal Yisrael began the work of the Mishkan. A people who had just been forgiven for the Eigel poured their energy into building Hashem a home. And then, before the plaster even dried, we're told to leave our own homes and move into a hut for a week.

🍺 A House Built on Trust, Not Walls

The Gemara in Sukkah brings a machlokes about what the "sukkos" of the pasuk actually were: Rabbi Eliezer says they were the Ananei HaKavod, the Clouds of Glory that surrounded and protected the nation in the midbar; Rabbi Akiva says they were literal huts. Either way, the point of the mitzvah is the same — we leave behind the house we built with our own hands, the one with locks and insulation and a mortgage, and we sit for a week under nothing but s'chach we can see the sky through. It's a physical rehearsal of bitachon: real security was never really about four solid walls.

🍺 Why This Is "Zman Simchaseinu"

This week's special Torah reading for Shabbos Chol Hamoed (Shemos 33:12–34:26) is the same passage where Hashem reveals the Yud Gimmel Middos HaRachamim to Moshe — the very attributes of mercy we leaned on all through Elul and the Yamim Noraim. Sukkos arrives as the joyful exhale after that whole intense stretch: the same Hashem Who forgave us on Yom Kippur is the One we now invite to sit with us, literally, under the same roof of clouds. That's why Sukkos, and not Rosh Hashana or Yom Kippur, is called Zman Simchaseinu — the season of our joy. Joy comes easiest right after you know, deeply, that you're forgiven and held.

May we all merit to feel truly held this Sukkos, walls or no walls. 🍺

Small tricks, big difference

Kitchen & Home Tips

Little hacks passed down from Bubbies, neighbors, and years of trial & error. Send us yours — we'll add them to the collection.

🍺
This week's featured tip

Line Your Serving Trays With a Baking Sheet for the Sukkah Walk

Before you start ferrying food from the kitchen to the sukkah, set each hot dish onto a rimmed baking sheet or a tray with a lip. It catches drips and steadies wobbly bowls on the walk outside, and if you're going down stairs or across a yard, the extra rim is the difference between a graceful entrance and a soup-covered path. Keep one tray by the door all of Yom Tov so it's never a search-and-rescue mission mid-meal.

🍺
Sukkah prep

Weigh down a paper tablecloth with binder clips

Skip the tape and clip a disposable tablecloth to the edges of your sukkah table with a few large binder clips. It won't rip when the wind picks up, and clips come off in two seconds for a quick changeover between meals.

🍌
Sukkah prep

Keep a rain plan ready

Fold a plastic tarp or painter's drop cloth to fit your s'chach and keep it folded by the door with bungee cords already attached. If rain starts mid-meal, you can cover the sukkah in under a minute instead of scrambling.

🕯
Time-saver

Pre-portion drinks in a cooler by the sukkah door

Fill a small cooler with bottled drinks and ice and keep it right outside the sukkah entrance. It saves dozens of trips back to the kitchen fridge over the course of Yom Tov, especially with guests in and out.

🛠
Organizing hack

Store sukkah decorations in a clearly labeled bin

Pack lulav and esrog holders, string lights, and wall decor into one bin labeled "Sukkos" right after Yom Tov ends. Next year's set-up takes ten minutes instead of an hour of searching the basement.

🍎
Produce hack

Keep apples crisp for weeks

Store apples in the crisper drawer wrapped loosely in a paper towel rather than piled loose in a bowl. They ripen slower and stay crunchy right through the Yom Tov season.

🧲
Make-ahead

Freeze soup in flat bags for the sukkah

Freeze extra soup in labeled, flat-lying freezer bags instead of containers. They thaw faster in a bowl of warm water and stack neatly in the freezer, so a hot bowl for the sukkah is never more than 20 minutes away.

🔌
Shine trick

Polish silver candlesticks with toothpaste

A dab of plain white toothpaste on a soft cloth buffs tarnish off silver in minutes. Rinse well and dry before candle lighting for a real shine.

🕯
Sukkah comfort

Keep a battery-powered fan or heater in the sukkah

Tishrei weather swings fast — a small battery or solar-powered fan for warm afternoons and a safe, sukkah-rated heater for cool nights means one less reason to rush meals.

🌽
Produce hack

Ripen hard avocados overnight

Tuck a hard avocado into a paper bag with a banana or apple and leave it on the counter overnight. The extra ethylene gas speeds up ripening so it's ready for salads or dips the next day.

📦
Organizing hack

Label Yom Tov dishes before you pack them away

As you unpack the dishes for the season, stick a small piece of painter's tape on each box listing exactly what's inside. Next year's unpacking (and next year's packing away) takes half the time.

For the little ones

Kids' Corner — No Electronics Needed

Ideas to save your sanity on long summer afternoons and hot Shabbos weekends. Grouped by situation — pick a tab, pick an idea, and go.

All ages

Memory tray challenge

Arrange 12 household items on a tray. Let everyone study it for one minute, then cover it up. Go around calling out items from memory — last one to forget wins.

Ages 5+

Bracha bee

Call out a food, kids race to say the correct bracha out loud. Get it right, you stay in; miss one, you sit out a round. Last one standing wins.

Ages 4+

Human knot

Everyone stands in a circle and grabs two different hands across the group. Working together (talking only, no letting go!) untangle back into one big circle.

Ages 3+

Balloon keep-it-up

Blow up a balloon before Shabbos starts. See how long the whole family can keep it from touching the floor — no hands allowed for the littlest ones is extra fun.

Ages 6+

Twenty questions

One person thinks of an object, person, or place from the parsha or around the house. Everyone else asks yes-or-no questions to guess it in twenty tries or fewer.

All ages

Decorate a tzedakah box

Keep a plain box and stickers, markers, and scraps of fabric on hand. Kids decorate their own pushke during the long afternoon, then start it off with a coin after Shabbos.

All ages

Backyard ice hunt

Freeze small toys in ice cube trays. Kids "excavate" them with spoons and warm water. Cools them down and buys you 45 minutes.

Ages 3+

Sidewalk chalk mural

Give each kid a section of the sidewalk. When done, walk the whole family through the "art gallery" and give awards.

Ages 5+

Lemonade stand

Set up out front. Real money, real customers. Kids learn making change, customer service, and marketing. Great mitzvah opportunity: donate to tzedakah.

All ages

The nature bingo card

Make a bingo card of things to find outside: red leaf, feather, three types of rocks, a bug, a puddle. Walk the block. First to bingo wins.

Ages 4+

Backyard obstacle course

Chairs, cushions, hula hoops, a broomstick. Have older kids design it. Time each other. Bonus: video the runs.

All ages

Sprinkler paintbrush

Give kids paintbrushes and buckets of water. Let them "paint" the fence, the driveway, the siding. Water evaporates — endless canvas.

All ages

Backyard scavenger hunt relay

Split into teams and hand out a list of ten outdoor items to find. First team back with everything wins — add a silly bonus item for extra laughs.

Ages 4+

Water balloon toss

Pair up and toss a water balloon back and forth, taking a step back after each successful catch. Last pair with an unbroken balloon wins.

Ages 4+

Sock puppet theater

Old socks + markers + buttons + yarn. Kids make puppets, then perform a show for the family after supper. Curtains: a blanket over two chairs.

Ages 3+

Restaurant at home

Kids write "menus," set the table, take orders. Serve real snacks. Amazing pretend-play. Great vocabulary building.

All ages

Fort under the dining table

Sheets, chairs, cushions, flashlights. Read books inside. Serve lunch in the fort as a special treat. Instant magic.

Ages 5+

Cardboard box marble maze

Cut open a cereal box or shoe box. Kids glue in cardboard pieces and straws to make a marble maze. Design + engineering + hours of play.

Ages 3+

Sensory bin

Big plastic tub + rice or dry beans + small toys hidden inside + scoops and cups. Kids can play for an hour. Contain to one room!

Ages 6+

Kid Iron Chef

Give kids 3 safe ingredients from the pantry. They "compete" to make a snack. You judge. Winner gets to pick supper for that night.

Ages 5+

Shadow puppet show

Cut simple shapes out of cardboard, tape them to skewers, and shine a flashlight at a bedsheet or wall. Kids write a short story and act it out in shadow.

Ages 4+

Indoor cup bowling

Set up ten plastic cups at the end of a hallway and roll a soft ball to knock them down. Keep score and play best of three between siblings.

Ages 1-3

Muffin tin sort

Give a muffin tin and a bowl of pom-poms, cheerios, or small blocks. Toddler "sorts" by color into the tin. 30 quiet minutes.

Ages 2-4

Water pouring station

Two cups, one bowl of water, tray underneath. Toddler pours water back and forth. Improves fine motor. Bring towels.

Ages 1-3

Sticker page

Sheet of stickers + blank paper. Toddlers can peel and stick for a shockingly long time. Buy stickers in bulk on Amazon.

Ages 2+

Play-dough tray

Homemade play-dough (flour, salt, water, food color) + cookie cutters + a rolling pin. Set them up at the table. Save the tray for next time.

Ages 1-2

Basket of "safe" kitchen tools

Wooden spoons, plastic measuring cups, silicone spatulas, small pot. Give them their own drawer in the kitchen. Endless fascination.

Ages 2-4

Painter's tape road map

Painter's tape on the floor becomes roads. Toy cars zoom around. Easy to peel up after. Endless configurations.

Ages 1-2

Peekaboo box

Cut a flap into a shoebox and hide a soft toy inside. Toddlers lift the flap again and again — the surprise never seems to get old.

Ages 1-3

Big cardboard box play

A large empty box becomes a car, a house, a boat — whatever a toddler imagines. Cut a window or door for even more mileage.

This week's portion, in brief

Parsha Short — Shabbos Chol Hamoed Sukkos

Shemos 33:12 – 34:26 · plus Megillas Koheles

Seeing What Can Be Seen, Right After Forgiveness

Because Shabbos falls out during Chol Hamoed this year, we set aside the regular weekly parsha and instead read a special selection: Moshe asking to see Hashem's glory, Hashem revealing the Yud Gimmel Middos HaRachamim (the Thirteen Attributes of Mercy) as Moshe stands in the cleft of the rock, and the covenant being renewed with a fresh set of luchos. It's the same passage that carried us through Selichos all of Elul — now read again, in the sukkah, as an answered prayer rather than a plea.

Ashkenazi custom also reads Megillas Koheles this Shabbos. Shlomo Hamelech's refrain — "hevel havalim, all is fleeting" — lands differently sitting in a hut with a roof of leaves than it would in a permanent home. Koheles isn't teaching us that nothing matters; it's teaching us to hold onto what actually lasts (Torah, mitzvos, relationships) a little more tightly, precisely because the sukkah reminds us how temporary everything else really is.

וָאֵרֶד ה' בֶּעָנָן וַּיִּתְיָ־צ־ב עִמּוֹShemos 34:5
For the Shabbos Table

Everything Is Temporary — That's the Whole Point

הֲבֵל הֲבָלִים אָמַר קֹהֶלֶת הֲבֵל הֲבָלִים הַּכֹּל הָבֶל

"Utter futility, said Koheles, utter futility, it is all futile" (Koheles 1:2). It's a strange line to read during Zman Simchaseinu, our happiest time of year — until you realize it's not a downer, it's a filter. Once you truly internalize that the money, the status, and even the walls of your house are temporary, you stop chasing them so anxiously and start noticing what was actually solid the whole time.

A short thought to share at the table: our sukkah only stands for a week, on purpose. Chazal wanted us to feel, once a year, what it's like to live without the illusion of permanence — and to notice that even then, surrounded by nothing but s'chach, we're still safe, still fed, still together. That feeling is meant to travel back inside with us when the walls go back up.

Halachos of Shabbos

Why the lulav stays in its holder this Shabbos

Is the Lulav Muktzeh on Shabbos Chol Hamoed?

כֹּּל הַּיּוֹם מִצְוָת לֻלָב חֻץ מִן הַּמִקְדָּש

Every day of Sukkos we take the daled minim and shake them in six directions — except Shabbos, even when Shabbos falls in the middle of Chol Hamoed like it does this year. The Gemara (Sukkah 42b) explains this isn't a Torah-level exemption; the mitzvah of lulav technically could apply on Shabbos too. Chazal held it back for one practical reason: they were concerned that someone not yet expert in the mitzvah might carry his lulav through a public domain on the way to a rav or teacher to learn how to shake it properly, forgetting in the moment that carrying itself is forbidden on Shabbos — the same underlying concern that keeps the shofar silent when Rosh Hashana falls on Shabbos.

Because the mitzvah doesn't apply that day, the Rama (Orach Chaim 658:2) rules that the lulav becomes muktzeh machmas gufo — muktzeh in its own right, the way a hammer or a pen is muktzeh on an ordinary Shabbos. A lulav has no other function; once its one job is off the table for the day, there's no permitted use left to justify picking it up. Practically, this means the daled minim should be set somewhere out of the way before Shabbos begins, since they may not be moved or handled once Shabbos starts, even just to clear space on the table.

One important exception: the esrog itself may be moved on Shabbos as long as it wasn't designated only for the mitzvah — if it's also fit to eat (many poskim are lenient here since an esrog is technically edible), it can retain food status and be moved like any other fruit. The lulav, hadassim, and aravos, which have no other use, remain fully muktzeh for the day. As always, the honey dish this year sits comfortably alongside a lulav that simply gets a well-earned day of rest.

Sourced from Sukkah 42b, Shulchan Aruch and Rama Orach Chaim 658:2. This is a general overview for learning purposes — for any specific situation, please ask your own Rav.

A weekly series

39 Melachos — Week 6: Tochen

The Melacha of Grinding

Tochen: Breaking Something Down Into Smaller Pieces

Tochen follows right after Borer in the Torah's bread-making sequence: once the kernels were sorted clean, they were ground into flour. The purpose of this melacha is taking something whole and breaking it down into smaller, more useful pieces — grain into flour, spices into powder, produce into a fine puree. It's easy to picture this only as literal milling, but the definition is broader than a millstone.

Food that grows from the ground and needs further preparation before eating is the classic case of Tochen; food that's ready to eat as-is generally isn't included, which is why slicing a block of cheese or shredding already-cooked meat by hand isn't a problem. What does raise a concern is using a tool designed specifically to grind or grate — a grater, a food processor's grinding blade, a mortar and pestle — even on food you're about to eat immediately, since the tool itself is what defines the act as Tochen rather than ordinary cutting.

Practically, this is why many families keep a plastic or glass grater set aside as "Shabbos-only" for foods that are permitted to be finely cut by hand (like grating a hard-boiled egg with a fork, or mashing a ripe avocado), while a metal cheese grater or garlic press stays off-limits. Crushing ice for a drink, grinding fresh pepper from a peppercorn mill, and running fresh herbs through a food processor are all classic Tochen concerns that come up at a Yom Tov table more than people realize.

Woman of Valor

This week's reflection

Rebbetzin Batsheva Kanievsky — A Door That Was Never Closed

Rebbetzin Batsheva Kanievsky, daughter of Rav Yosef Shalom Elyashiv and wife of Rav Chaim Kanievsky, could have spent her days quietly immersed in the Torah world surrounding her. Instead, from morning until night, her modest Bnei Brak apartment functioned as an open door for anyone who needed one — Torah-observant, traditional, or completely unaffiliated, it made no difference to her. Thousands came each week simply to ask for a bracha or share what was weighing on their heart.

What made her hachnasas orchim remarkable wasn't grand gestures; it was the way she made every single visitor, waiting on line for hours, feel like the only person in the room when their turn finally came. She listened fully, answered warmly, and sent people home lighter than they arrived. When she passed away in 2011, over 50,000 people filled the streets of Bnei Brak for her levaya — a testament to just how many doors she had personally kept open. Sukkos asks us to widen our own doorways for guests, real and spiritual; Rebbetzin Kanievsky spent a lifetime showing what that looks like practiced every single day.

In honor of Rebbetzin Batsheva Kanievsky a"h · whose home was open to all — and of every Staten Island woman opening her sukkah to guests this Yom Tov
A thought to carry with us

This Week's Chinuch Nugget

This week

Real Security Was Never About the Walls

We leave a solid house for a hut with a roof you can see stars through — and the Torah calls that our happiest week of the year. Kids absorb security from how calm we stay, not from how much stuff surrounds them. Try sitting in the sukkah this week and naming out loud one thing that makes you feel safe that isn't a "thing" at all — Shabbos, family, tefillah. It teaches kids where real security actually comes from.
Parenting Wisdom · Shabbos Chol Hamoed Sukkos
A second thought

Every Guest Gets the Same Warm Welcome

Each night of Sukkos we symbolically invite an Ushpiz — Avraham, Yitzchak, Yaakov, and the others — into our sukkah, one after another, each with full honor. Kids notice whether a guest who's "less exciting" gets a warmer or cooler welcome than a favorite visitor. This week, let a child help greet every guest who walks into your sukkah the exact same way, big simcha or small, and talk about why every guest deserves that same welcome.
