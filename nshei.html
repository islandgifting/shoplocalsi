Dvar Torah · Parshas Shoftim

Why the Torah Says Justice Twice

Parshas Shoftim opens with an instruction that almost sounds redundant: "Tzedek, tzedek tirdof" — justice, justice shall you pursue. Why say the same word twice?

Why the Torah Says Justice Twice

"Judges and officers shall you appoint for yourself in all your gates... and they shall judge the people with righteous judgment." Then, almost immediately: "Tzedek, tzedek tirdof" — justice, justice, shall you pursue.

Parshas Shoftim opens the final stretch of Sefer Devarim with the mitzvah to set up courts and law enforcement — shoftim v'shotrim — in every city. Just a few pesukim later comes one of the most quoted lines in the entire Torah, doubled for emphasis: not just "pursue justice," but "justice, justice, shall you pursue."

⚖️ Why the Repetition?

Chazal offer a few answers, and they're not mutually exclusive. Some say the doubling teaches that the pursuit of justice must itself be just — you can't reach a fair outcome through unfair means. Others say it means: pursue justice whether it benefits you or costs you, whether the case is large or small, whether the person across from you is a stranger or your closest friend. Justice doesn't get to be selective.

Rashi notes the word tirdof — "pursue" — implies actively chasing after it, even going out of your way, even walking to a better court in another town if the one nearby isn't reliable. Justice isn't just something we wait to receive; it's something we're meant to run after.

🏡 What It Looks Like at Home

Most of us aren't sitting as dayanim, but the pursuit of tzedek shows up constantly in ordinary life — judging a friend favorably before assuming the worst, being honest in business even when no one would catch the shortcut, being fair to each child even when it's easier to play favorites, speaking up gently when something isn't right instead of looking away.

Shoftim falls right at the start of Elul, and that timing isn't an accident. As we begin the work of cheshbon hanefesh — taking honest stock of ourselves — "justice, justice" is also a call to judge ourselves fairly: not too harshly, and not by looking away from what needs fixing either.

May we merit to pursue justice honestly — with others, and with ourselves. ⚖️

Small tricks, big difference

Kitchen & Home Tips

Little hacks passed down from Bubbies, neighbors, and years of trial & error. Send us yours — we'll add them to the collection.

🌱
Elul is here

Stock up on honey before the rush

Rosh Hashana honey sells out fast once Elul is in full swing. Grab a jar or two now while the shelves are full and prices are normal.

🍱
Freezer trick

Double the soup, freeze half

Anytime you make soup, double the recipe and freeze half in a flat, labeled bag. It thaws faster and gives you an instant weeknight dinner later in Elul.

🌱
Produce hack

Keep herbs fresh for two weeks

Trim the stems and stand herbs in a cup of water like flowers, then loosely cover with a plastic bag and refrigerate. Parsley and cilantro stay crisp far longer than in their original packaging.

🧸
Cleaning hack

Sanitize your sponge in the microwave

Wet the sponge fully and microwave for one minute. It kills most of the bacteria hiding inside and makes the sponge smell fresh again — no need to toss it as often.

🥛
Pantry fix

Stop rice and salt from clumping

Drop a few grains of raw rice into your salt shaker or sugar canister. The rice absorbs excess moisture and keeps everything pouring freely, even on humid days.

🧲
Make-ahead

Freeze muffin batter, bake fresh

Portion unbaked muffin or coffee cake batter into a lined tin and freeze. Bake straight from frozen (add 5-8 extra minutes) for oven-fresh Shabbos morning treats.

🥚
Kitchen trick

Peel hard-boiled eggs in seconds

Shock eggs in an ice bath right after boiling, then gently roll each one on the counter to crackle the shell all over. It slips off in one or two pieces.

🔌
Shine trick

Polish silver candlesticks with toothpaste

A dab of plain white toothpaste on a soft cloth buffs tarnish off silver in minutes. Rinse well and dry before candle lighting for a real shine.

For the little ones

Kids' Corner — No Electronics Needed

Ideas to save your sanity on long summer afternoons and hot Shabbos weekends. Grouped by situation — pick a tab, pick an idea, and go.

All ages

Memory tray challenge

Arrange 12 household items on a tray. Let everyone study it for one minute, then cover it up. Go around calling out items from memory — last one to forget wins.

Ages 5+

Bracha bee

Call out a food, kids race to say the correct bracha out loud. Get it right, you stay in; miss one, you sit out a round. Last one standing wins.

Ages 4+

Human knot

Everyone stands in a circle and grabs two different hands across the group. Working together (talking only, no letting go!) untangle back into one big circle.

Ages 3+

Balloon keep-it-up

Blow up a balloon before Shabbos starts. See how long the whole family can keep it from touching the floor — no hands allowed for the littlest ones is extra fun.

All ages

Backyard ice hunt

Freeze small toys in ice cube trays. Kids "excavate" them with spoons and warm water. Cools them down and buys you 45 minutes.

Ages 3+

Sidewalk chalk mural

Give each kid a section of the sidewalk. When done, walk the whole family through the "art gallery" and give awards.

Ages 5+

Lemonade stand

Set up out front. Real money, real customers. Kids learn making change, customer service, and marketing. Great mitzvah opportunity: donate to tzedakah.

All ages

The nature bingo card

Make a bingo card of things to find outside: red leaf, feather, three types of rocks, a bug, a puddle. Walk the block. First to bingo wins.

Ages 4+

Backyard obstacle course

Chairs, cushions, hula hoops, a broomstick. Have older kids design it. Time each other. Bonus: video the runs.

All ages

Sprinkler paintbrush

Give kids paintbrushes and buckets of water. Let them "paint" the fence, the driveway, the siding. Water evaporates — endless canvas.

Ages 4+

Sock puppet theater

Old socks + markers + buttons + yarn. Kids make puppets, then perform a show for the family after supper. Curtains: a blanket over two chairs.

Ages 3+

Restaurant at home

Kids write "menus," set the table, take orders. Serve real snacks. Amazing pretend-play. Great vocabulary building.

All ages

Fort under the dining table

Sheets, chairs, cushions, flashlights. Read books inside. Serve lunch in the fort as a special treat. Instant magic.

Ages 5+

Cardboard box marble maze

Cut open a cereal box or shoe box. Kids glue in cardboard pieces and straws to make a marble maze. Design + engineering + hours of play.

Ages 3+

Sensory bin

Big plastic tub + rice or dry beans + small toys hidden inside + scoops and cups. Kids can play for an hour. Contain to one room!

Ages 6+

Kid Iron Chef

Give kids 3 safe ingredients from the pantry. They "compete" to make a snack. You judge. Winner gets to pick supper for that night.

Ages 1-3

Muffin tin sort

Give a muffin tin and a bowl of pom-poms, cheerios, or small blocks. Toddler "sorts" by color into the tin. 30 quiet minutes.

Ages 2-4

Water pouring station

Two cups, one bowl of water, tray underneath. Toddler pours water back and forth. Improves fine motor. Bring towels.

Ages 1-3

Sticker page

Sheet of stickers + blank paper. Toddlers can peel and stick for a shockingly long time. Buy stickers in bulk on Amazon.

Ages 2+

Play-dough tray

Homemade play-dough (flour, salt, water, food color) + cookie cutters + a rolling pin. Set them up at the table. Save the tray for next time.

Ages 1-2

Basket of "safe" kitchen tools

Wooden spoons, plastic measuring cups, silicone spatulas, small pot. Give them their own drawer in the kitchen. Endless fascination.

Ages 2-4

Painter's tape road map

Painter's tape on the floor becomes roads. Toy cars zoom around. Easy to peel up after. Endless configurations.

This week's portion, in brief

Parsha Short — Shoftim

Devarim 16:18 – 21:9

Judges, Justice & the Start of Elul

Parshas Shoftim opens with the command to appoint judges and officers in every city, followed by the famous double command: "Tzedek, tzedek tirdof" — justice, justice shall you pursue. The parsha goes on to lay out the laws of a Jewish king, the special status of the Levi'im, the cities of refuge for someone who kills accidentally, and rules for going out to war — including the surprising instruction to first offer peace.

The parsha closes with eglah arufah: when a body is found in a field and the killer is unknown, the elders of the nearest city must publicly declare "our hands did not spill this blood," taking communal responsibility for a stranger's welfare even at the town's edge.

Shoftim always falls right around Rosh Chodesh Elul — a fitting pair. Just as we're commanded to seek justice from the courts, Elul asks us to sit as both judge and defendant with ourselves, honestly.

Halachos of Shabbos

Everyday Halacha

Muktzah: What You Can (and Can't) Move

Muktzah refers to items that are "set aside" from use on Shabbos, and while the topic has many categories, a few common household items come up every week. A pen, phone, or car key is muktzah machmas chisaron kis (its main use is entirely weekday), and may not be moved at all — not even to get it out of the way.

Money, receipts, and unopened mail fall into the same category. A raw egg, uncut fruit still on the tree, or a candle that's currently lit are muktzah machmas gufo — their present state simply isn't usable on Shabbos.

There's good news too: many things people assume are muktzah actually aren't. A siddur, a Shabbos-appropriate toy, a serving spoon, or a cloth napkin were all set aside for permitted uses and may be handled freely, even just to tidy up.

Sourced from the Shulchan Aruch (Orach Chaim 308) and Mishna Berurah. This is a general overview for learning purposes — for any specific situation, please ask your own Rav.

39 Melachos — Week 2: Borer

The Melacha of Selecting

Borer: Separating Good from Bad

Borer means taking something out of a mixture and setting it aside — the classic example is separating unwanted stones or chaff from grain. In the Mishkan, this was done while preparing dyes: after threshing, any remaining pebbles or debris had to be picked out by hand. That simple act of sorting is why Borer appears on the list of 39 melachos, and why so many everyday kitchen moments touch on it.

To separate something on Shabbos without violating Borer, three conditions must all be met: you're taking the food you want away from what you don't want (not the reverse), you're doing it by hand (not with a strainer, sieve, or other tool made for separating), and it's for immediate use — right before eating, not to set aside for later.

A few familiar examples: when eating fish, it's best to remove the bones from your mouth as you go, or eat around them, rather than picking bones out of the fish first. Watermelon seeds should be spit out while eating, or shaken/picked out right before a bite, not sorted out in advance. A teabag should be lifted out of a cup with a spoon, not squeezed and set aside. In each case, the same three conditions decide whether it's permitted.

Sourced from the Mishna Berurah, Shemiras Shabbos Kehilchasa, and contemporary poskim. This is a general overview for learning purposes — for any specific situation, please ask your own Rav.

Woman of Valor

This week's reflection

The Quiet Work of Judging Favorably

Parshas Shoftim commands us to pursue justice — and every woman who has ever bit her tongue instead of assuming the worst about a friend, given a struggling neighbor the benefit of the doubt, or mediated a squabble between siblings with real fairness instead of picking a favorite, has lived that mitzvah in miniature, all week long.

Dan l'kaf zechus — judging others favorably — rarely gets applause. It happens quietly: choosing not to repeat a story that isn't yours to tell, giving the carpool mom who's late the benefit of the doubt, believing a child's explanation before assuming the worst. As Elul begins, it's worth remembering that the same fair-mindedness we're asked to extend to others is worth turning inward too — being honest with ourselves without being unkind to ourselves.

In honor of every Staten Island woman quietly pursuing justice at home this week
A thought to carry with us

This Week's Chinuch Nugget

This week

See the Choice, Choose Today

Parshas Re'eh opens with Moshe telling Klal Yisrael, "Re'eh Anochi nosein lifneichem hayom bracha uklalah" — See, I am placing before you today a blessing and a curse. The key word is "today": this wasn't a one-time decision made back at Har Sinai, it's a fresh choice offered every single day. Kids don't need to feel stuck being defined by yesterday's mistake or yesterday's bad mood. This week, try asking at bedtime: "What's one bracha you chose today?" — it reframes the whole day around the good choices, not just what went wrong.
